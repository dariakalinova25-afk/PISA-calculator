How many years of schooling separate students from less and more advantaged families?

In 2025, 15-year-old students took the international PISA science test, run by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). We compared the results of children from different family backgrounds and translated the score gap into years of schooling.

How many PISA points ≈ 1 year of schooling

In Australia, by ≈ 4.0 years of schooling.

The social issue

On average, children from less advantaged families score noticeably lower in PISA: in the four countries we compared, the gap is between 4 and 5.3 years of schooling. From the perspective of conflict theory, unequal access to resources (parents' education, books, tutors, stronger schools) passes inequality from one generation to the next.

Possible solutions

  • Extra funding for schools with more children from less advantaged families.
  • Free tutoring and after-school learning support.
  • Early childhood development and parent support programmes.

How we calculated

Method

Students in each country were divided into four groups (quartiles) using the weighted distribution of the ESCS index. For each group, we calculated the weighted (W_FSTUWT) mean science score separately for each of the 10 plausible values (PV1SCIE–PV10SCIE) and then averaged these 10 estimates.

The score gap was divided by the number of points students gain on average over one school year. The main estimate, about 20 points (≈ 0.2 SD), comes from OECD research (Avvisati & Givord, 2021). Depending on the country, the annual gain ranges from roughly 10 to 25 points, so the calculator shows a range. The conversion into years of schooling is approximate and illustrative.

Methods we considered and why we chose this one

“40 points ≈ 1 year” (older OECD estimate)

What it is: early OECD reports based on PISA 2000–2003 data.

Drawback: later, more precise estimates are half as large, so this figure halves the gap in years.

Not chosen

“20 points ≈ 1 year” (current OECD estimate)

What it is: Avvisati & Givord (OECD, 2021) compared students of the same age who are in different grades because of their birth date (18 countries). Over one school year, scores rise on average by about 1/5 of a standard deviation, i.e. ≈ 20 PISA points.

Advantages: estimated on PISA data and specifically for 15-year-olds; the OECD uses it in its own reports.

Drawback: the value differs by country and has wide confidence intervals: from about 0.1 SD (Malaysia) to 0.25 SD or more (Austria, Scotland).

Chosen as the main estimate

Gap in standard deviations (effect size)

What it is: the gap is divided by the score SD (≈ 100 in PISA) and compared with empirical benchmarks (Hill, Bloom, Black & Lipsey, 2008).

Advantage: statistically sound and comparable across tests.

Drawback: “0.8 SD” is hard to understand without a statistics background. The authors themselves stress there is no universal rule for interpreting it.

Used as the basis for the “20 points” estimate

Percentiles

What it is: how many percentiles higher a student would rank if the gap were closed.

Advantage: Baird & Pane (2019) find this the most reliable option and “years of learning” the least stable, because it depends heavily on the chosen conversion factor.

Drawback: conveys the scale of the social issue less clearly.

We took the critique into account: that is why we show a range

LAYS (World Bank)

What it is: “learning-adjusted years of schooling”: expected years of school × (harmonized test score / 625).

Drawback: designed to compare countries with each other, not groups of students within a country.

Not suitable for comparing student groups

Our conclusion. Years of schooling show the scale of inequality most clearly, so we kept them. But given the critique by Baird & Pane (2019) and the differences between countries, we do not present a single number as exact. The main estimate is 20 points per year (OECD, 2021), always shown with a range of 10 to 25 points.

Glossary
OECD
Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. An international organisation of mostly high-income countries that runs the PISA test.
PISA
Programme for International Student Assessment: a test for 15-year-old students that the OECD runs every few years. We used the science results.
PISA score
The test result. The scale is built so that the OECD average is about 480–500, and most students score between 400 and 600.
ESCS
Index of economic, social and cultural status: how well-resourced and educated a student's family is (parents' education and occupation, books, a computer, a room of one's own at home).
Groups (quartiles)
Students in each country are split into 4 equal parts by ESCS: least advantaged 25%, below average, above average, most advantaged 25%.
Year of schooling
Over one school year, a student gains ≈ 20 PISA points on average.
Weight (W_FSTUWT)
Shows how many students in the country each test-taker “represents”. Using it makes results reflect the whole country more accurately.
PV1SCIE–PV10SCIE
10 plausible values of each student's score (this is how PISA accounts for measurement error). We average all ten.
SD (standard deviation)
How much students' scores vary around the average. In PISA it is ≈ 100 points.
